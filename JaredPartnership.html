Heads of Terms

The desert recovery-housing partnership. Drawn from the fifteen points now agreed in the question matrix, with the open commercial questions marked rather than hidden.

1 Parties and vehicle

1.1  The parties

Samuel Barlow provides capital and builds the reporting and financial oversight. Jared sources, acquires, renovates and operates the properties and the associated businesses on the ground.

1.2  Ownership vehicle

Agreed

Samuel obtains a US ITIN and each property is held by a US LLC, so Samuel is not on title personally. This is to be structured on qualified cross-border tax advice before any capital moves, to address FIRPTA, US filing and estate-tax exposure.

Matrix: "I shouldn't be on title personally" — agreed.

1.3  The venture

Two linked workstreams: a kept portfolio of recovery-housing rentals, and a flip portfolio built and sold to recycle capital. The base-case property is Jared's "Project #2" model: acquire at $150,000–$200,000, finished value $250,000–$300,000, ten beds. Project #1 is treated as a one-off and not relied on for the model.

2 Capital and security

2.1  Amount and form

Agreed

Samuel commits $40,000, advanced as a recorded loan secured by a lien on the relevant property, not an unsecured contribution. The lien sits in second position behind the hard-money lender; the exact position is to be confirmed per deal.

Matrix: "My money in as a recorded loan/lien" — agreed.

2.2  Released against milestones

Agreed

Capital is released property by property against agreed milestones (property identified, contracts signed, renovation budget agreed), not in a single lump. No money is spent or committed without Samuel's prior approval.

Matrix: "Money released property-by-property against milestones" — agreed ("no money will be spent… that isn't approved prior").

2.3  Overruns are the operator's, not a call on your capital

Renegotiate

Samuel's capital is capped at $40,000 and is never subject to a further call. Cost overruns are the operator's responsibility, funded from the project, the hard money or Jared's margin, never shared with Samuel. Each renovation budget carries a stated contingency (10–15%), so an "overrun" means only what exceeds an already-padded figure. The party who controls and self-performs the build carries the risk of it running over.

Why this changes. Jared agreed 50/50, but with a hard $40,000 cap a shared overrun is an open-ended call Samuel cannot meet, and it could take half his capital on one project. Jared's own position is "there won't be an overrun" — so bearing them costs him nothing if he means it. His answer is a free test of how firm the budget really is.

Supersedes the earlier "Who funds an overrun? — agreed 50/50."

2.4  Hard-money terms disclosed first

Agreed

Samuel sees the hard-money terms (rate, points, term length, guarantees, overrun behaviour) before committing to each deal.

Matrix: "Sight of the hard-money terms before I commit" — agreed.

3 The return, and the $4,000 target

3.1  Capital comes back first

Agreed

There is no profit split until expenses, including Samuel's capital, are recovered. Distributable profit is only what remains after that.

Matrix: "Capital repaid… before profits split 50/50" — agreed ("no profit until expenses… including capital in are recovered").

3.2  Priority distribution to Samuel, targeting $4,000/month

Open

From available net cashflow, Samuel receives a priority monthly distribution ahead of Jared's profit share, with a target of $4,000/month. On the current figures this target is reached in stages, not immediately:

Feasibility, stated plainly. A guaranteed $4,000/month from month 3 is not supported by $40,000 on the current model. One house yields Samuel roughly $2,000–$2,500; $4,000 needs about two stabilised houses, and $40,000 funds one house plus one flip-in-progress. $4,000/month becomes realistic in months 6–12 as each ~$50,000 flip recycles into another rental — on the path to the $10,000/month originally discussed.

3.3  A hard floor is a separate ask

Open

If Samuel requires a contractual $4,000/month floor from month 3 regardless of cashflow, that is Jared underwriting the gap personally (from the other businesses). It converts Samuel's equity risk into a fixed obligation Jared owes. It is a legitimate term, but it needs either more capital up front (~$70,000–$80,000) or Jared's explicit personal guarantee, and must be negotiated as such rather than assumed.

3.4  Split above the priority

Once capital is returned and the priority distribution is met, remaining profit is split 50/50.

3.5  Your return does not depend on your involvement

Open

Two different things, split by what they pay for. The investment return — capital back, the priority distribution and the equity share — is payment for money and risk, and is not contingent on Samuel doing any work or staying involved for any period. It is protected by the lien and the priority regardless. The business support fee is payment for work (the reporting and oversight Elämä provides) and continues for as long as that service is provided, subject to notice. The effect: ending Samuel's involvement can stop the fee, but cannot touch his investment economics.

4 Governance and decisions

4.1  Joint decisions

Open

A short list of decisions needs both parties: taking on debt, and buying or selling a property. Everything operational is Jared's alone, without asking. The exact list is to be finalised.

Matrix: "'both of us' decision list" — Jared open to it, sees a tiebreaker as the resolution.

4.2  Deadlock and a third voice

Open

A deadlock clause is agreed: either party may name a price, and the other chooses to buy or sell at it. Jared prefers, in addition, a third person as tiebreaker and has suggested Brett taking a small stake.

Decide carefully. A third party changes the 50/50 and introduces a new dependency. Who Brett is, the size and terms of his stake, and his genuine neutrality all need pinning down before this is a protection rather than a complication.

Matrix: "deadlock clause" — agreed, third-person tiebreaker proposed.

4.3  Exit

Agreed

A route out for either party at a formula agreed up front.

Matrix: "An exit formula for either party" — agreed.

4.4  Decisions that cannot be done around you

Open

Beyond debt and buying or selling property, these require Samuel's consent: winding up, dissolving or liquidating the company; selling all or substantially all of the assets; transferring any property or the business to another entity either party controls; and issuing new equity that would dilute Samuel. These are precisely the moves used to structure a minority partner out, so they are named rather than left to trust.

4.5  If it ends, you are first in line

Open

Because Samuel's money is a secured loan (clause 2.1), on any winding-up he is a secured creditor paid before any equity. The wind-up order is: secured loan first, then any unpaid priority distribution, then the equity split. If Jared sells his stake or the assets, Samuel has a tag-along right to exit on the same terms. Together with the exit formula and deadlock clause, the worst outcome available to either side is a buy-out at a fair price, not a squeeze-out.

5.1  Visibility

Agreed

Read-only bank access for Samuel on every account, and Xero or QuickBooks from day one with full access. Samuel builds the reconciliation and reporting; Jared produces no numbers by hand.

Matrix: both agreed.

5.2  Controls

Agreed
  • No cash rent. Everything by ACH, Zelle or resident portal, which also builds each resident a mortgage file for the homeownership path.
  • An independent bookkeeper does the entry, not Jared.
  • Two signatures on payments above an agreed threshold.

Matrix: all three agreed.

5.3  Related-party work

Agreed

Where a business either party owns does work for the venture (for example Jared's team self-performing the renovation), the margin is agreed in advance and in writing, not discussed afterwards.

Matrix: "Margins on payments to businesses either of us owns" — agreed.

6.4  References

Agreed

Two references each way, including one relationship that did not end well. Samuel goes first.

Matrix: "References in both directions" — agreed.
